\section{Architecture Protocols}
\label{sec:protocols}

Six edges join the seven nodes of \cref{sec:components} and the browser, and no two of
them carry the same protocol. \Cref{tab:edges} is the map; the reasons for the three
middleware choices are argued in \cref{sec:why-middleware}, and the messages on each edge
are specified in \cref{sec:api}. What this section adds is the shape of the map: which
party opens each connection, which way authority flows, and what was deliberately kept off
an edge.

\subsection{Client-facing edges}
\label{sec:protocols-client}

The browser talks to two servers, and that is the first decision on this map. Pages come
from the back office over HTTP, rendered by servlets and JSP (\cref{sec:ssr}); everything
that happens at a station, reserving, watching a charge, stopping it, goes over a WebSocket
opened \emph{directly to the station node}. The alternative was to route driver traffic
through Tomcat, which would have kept one origin and one authentication scheme. We rejected
it because it puts the web tier on the path of every reservation and every live frame: a
Tomcat restart would have blanked every charging page in the network, and the station could
no longer keep a site working while the back office is down. The price is a second
authentication: the back office mints a JWT, the station verifies it locally and never
calls back (\cref{sec:api-jwt}).

The charge point has an edge of its own, kept apart from the driver's on purpose. Driver
and hardware are two parties that must never be confused: one asks and the other reports.
Matching a reported cable against a reservation is the station's job
(\cref{sec:domain-rules}). The charge point dials the station, one socket per connector, as
real equipment dials an operator's backend from behind a site network; the station never
dials hardware. WebSocket with JSON on this edge is what OCPP itself uses, so the emulator
speaks a subset of a real protocol (\cref{sec:api-ws-cp}).

\subsection{Internal edges}
\label{sec:protocols-internal}

Between Erlang nodes the protocol is distributed Erlang. A station addresses the
coordinator as \texttt{\{vs\_coord\_srv, Node\}} and the coordinator addresses a station as
\texttt{\{vs\_claim\_client, Node\}}, registered names on both sides, so nobody holds an
address; the same connection carries \texttt{nodedown}, so a crashed peer becomes a message
in a mailbox (\cref{sec:detectors}). The direction of the edge is fixed: stations call and the
coordinator answers; a revocation travels in the reply to the next renewal. The
coordinator is never on the path of a charge, which is what lets a coordination outage cost
only new reservations (\cref{sec:api-claim}).

The back office joins the same cluster as a hidden Erlang node through JInterface. It
receives the station directory and the identity of the leader as they change, and it pushes
suspensions back; no page of the web tier ever asks the cluster a question at request time
(\cref{sec:api-bridge}). Being hidden means it takes no part in the quorum: the web tier
must never influence who leads.

MySQL is reached from two runtimes, and the rule that makes that safe is written at the top
of \texttt{schema.sql}: \textbf{one writer per table}. The station writes one row, the
closed session, through \texttt{mysql-otp} (it also reads the vehicle-to-account mapping).
Java owns the other tables (users, vehicles, notifications) and the cost of a session
after the fact. The no-show counter lives in
\texttt{users} and is written by Java alone: a station reports the no-show to the
coordinator, which relays it. A counter incremented from several nodes would have been a
second contended object, and the schema forbids it.

What is kept off the internal edges is as telling as what travels on them: no replicated
log between the coordinators (\cref{sec:rebuild}), and no station talking to another.
